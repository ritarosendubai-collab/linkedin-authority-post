# Caption And Reach Mechanics

## The Caption's Job

When a carousel carries the detail, the caption is not a summary of the carousel. Its job is to stop the scroll and set up the swipe. Shorter than feels right.

If the caption fully explains the post, nobody swipes, and a carousel that nobody swipes performs worse than a single image would have.

## Shape

1. **The stake, in one line.** Ideally the same hook as the cover, phrased differently. This is the only line guaranteed to be read — everything after the truncation point is opt-in.
2. **Context, two or three lines.** What it is, who issued it, when it takes effect, and one honest observation about it (that almost nobody has prepared, that it replaced something weaker, that it is unusual).
3. **The list.** The headline protections as short standalone lines. No bullets needed — line breaks do the work and read cleaner on mobile.
4. **The turn.** "Here's the part most people miss." Then the qualifier, in plain language, with its consequence spelled out.
5. **The practical close.** What the reader should do or have ready. Concrete. Name the item most people get wrong.
6. **Optional considered line.** A fair observation about the change itself — see `tone-calibration.md`.
7. **Pointer to the carousel.** One line: full breakdown in the carousel, reference on every slide.
8. **Source line.** Instrument name, number, issuer domain.
9. **One question.** Directed at a specific group, answerable in a few words.
10. **Three hashtags.** Not fifteen.

## The Closing Question

A question that anyone could answer gets answered by nobody. Aim it at the group whose job actually changes, and make it answerable in one word.

"What do you think?" produces nothing. "Are your teams ready for a three-day turnaround on the 13th — yes or no?" produces replies, because it is addressed to a specific role, it is binary, and answering it signals expertise rather than requiring an essay.

## Link Placement

LinkedIn suppresses reach on posts containing external links. Two options, and the choice is a real trade-off:

- **Link in the post, near the end** — three or four lines from the bottom. The citation is visible, and the reach penalty is limited by position. Use when credibility matters more than reach, which for a regulatory post it usually does.
- **Link in your own first comment** — no penalty on the post. The slides still carry the article references, so the post remains verifiable without it. Use when reach is the priority.

Do not put a link mid-caption. Worst of both.

## Document Post Vs Image Post

- **Document post (PDF upload)** — renders as a swipeable viewer, typically reaches further, and keeps the swipe interaction that makes a carousel worth building.
- **Image post (multiple PNGs)** — more reliable rendering, simpler, safer if the PDF has font-embedding problems.

Default to the document post; keep the PNGs ready as a fallback. Deliver both so the choice stays with the user.

Verify the PDF renders correctly in the LinkedIn preview before publishing — embedded-font failures and colour shifts show up there and not in a local viewer.

## The First Hour

Early comments drive distribution more than anything in the caption. A post with strong impressions and almost no comments has been capped by its own comment count — the reach was there and the engagement signal was not.

So: publish when you can spend the following hour replying. Reply to every comment properly, not with an emoji. If the audience is regional, publish mid-morning on a working day in that market — and check the local working week rather than assuming Monday–Friday.

Tell the user this explicitly when delivering. It is the highest-leverage thing they control and the easiest to forget.

## Benchmarking Honestly

If the user has figures from previous posts, use them as the comparison — their own baseline is the only meaningful one. Report impressions against comment count, because that ratio is what diagnoses a capped post.

Do not predict reach. Do not claim a format "gets 3× engagement" without a source. Format guidance here is mechanical and platform-behavioural; anything numeric needs either the user's own data or a cited study with a date.
