# Audience And Funnel

## Three Different Questions

"Who is this for" hides three separate questions with different answers. Answer all three, in `audience-note.md`, before the post goes out.

**Who it genuinely helps.** The people whose situation improves because they read it. For a regulatory post this is usually the protected party — the business owner, the founder, the finance manager who can act on a specific line item. Name the slides that serve them.

**Who will actually engage.** Often a different group entirely: the people whose *job changes* because of the rule. Compliance staff, relationship managers, operations leads, the advisers around the protected party. They comment because the post is about their work, and because answering signals competence to their own network. Aim the closing question at this group — they are the ones who reply.

**Who it is not for.** State this plainly. Companies above the size threshold, consumers covered by a different regime, anyone outside the jurisdiction. Knowing the non-audience keeps the copy from hedging to cover people it was never written for, and hedged copy is what makes regulatory posts unreadable.

The gap between group one and group two is not a problem. It is why the post travels: one group finds it useful, a different group finds it professionally relevant, and a third — the advisers — reshare it because it is a conversation they are already having with clients.

## The Funnel Caveat

Say this out loud when it is true, because it is easy to mistake a well-performing post for a well-aimed one.

An authority post earns reach, credibility, and attention from a professional audience. That is not the same as reaching buyers. If the topic is adjacent to what the user actually sells rather than aligned with it, the post is top-of-funnel authority, not lead generation — and it will attract an audience that is engaged but not purchasing.

That is a legitimate thing to publish. It is not a failure. But the user should know which one they are getting before they publish, not after they count the leads.

**So name it.** In `audience-note.md`, state: this post attracts *X* audience; the user sells to *Y*; here is the overlap, honestly assessed.

## Plan The Follow-Up First

The value of an authority post is the attention it earns. Attention decays fast, and it decays whether or not anything is pointed at it.

If the post will attract an audience adjacent to the buyer, identify — before publishing — the follow-up post that points that attention at something the user can convert. Usually a topic sitting closer to the buyer: the same rigour, a subject the buyer actually has budget for.

The sequence works because the first post buys credibility with a wider audience and the second spends it. Publishing the first without having planned the second wastes the reach.

Record the proposed follow-up topic in `audience-note.md`.

## Reading The User's ICP

Ask, or infer from their previous posts and their business:

- what they sell, and to whom;
- which industries, seniorities, and geographies;
- whether the goal for this specific post is reach, credibility, or leads — they are different posts.

If the stated goal is leads and the topic is adjacent rather than aligned, say so before building. It is a much cheaper conversation before the deck exists than after.
